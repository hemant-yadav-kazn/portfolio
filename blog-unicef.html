Field Log: UNICEF

Thirty Seconds.
To Trust.

"Nobody stops for a stranger. They stop for a story."

In 2020, I spent my days on the street with a clipboard, a lanyard, and a script I hated at first. Fundraising for UNICEF sounds noble from the outside. From the inside, it's a numbers game built on rejection—dozens of "no thank yous" for every person who slows down.

I learned fast that the job had nothing to do with the pitch and everything to do with the first THREE SECONDS. Eye contact, a genuine smile, a question instead of a request. People don't donate to a script; they donate to a person who seems to actually see them.

The pitch is 10% of the conversation

By the end of the campaign, our team had raised over $38,000 USD in direct contributions. Not through pressure tactics, but through what I started calling the 30-SECOND PITCH—a compressed version of trust-building that had to work in the time it takes someone to walk past you on a sidewalk.

The framework was simple, even if executing it under pressure wasn't. Hook them with a question, not a fact. Humanize the cause with one specific story instead of a statistic. Hand off the decision back to them—nobody likes being cornered, so the best pitches always left an easy exit.

Rejection Was the Curriculum

I got told no more times in that one summer than in the rest of my life combined. Doors closed, people crossed the street, a few were outright rude. It stopped being personal once I saw the pattern: rejection wasn't a verdict on me, it was just DATA. Every "no" told me something about timing, tone, or approach that I could adjust on the very next conversation.

That shift—treating a hard "no" as feedback instead of failure—is the single most useful thing I took out of that job. It's the same instinct I now bring to sales conversations at Amway and to pitching KAZN to a skeptical brand owner. The stakes are different. The psychology is identical.

Every rejection was a note for the next pitch

The best donors weren't the ones with the most money—they were the ones who felt like they'd been part of a real exchange, not a transaction. I'd rather have a $20 donor who remembers my name than a $200 donor who forgets me the second I walk away.

Persuasion, done honestly, isn't manipulation. It's just clarity delivered with enough warmth that someone's willing to hear it.
